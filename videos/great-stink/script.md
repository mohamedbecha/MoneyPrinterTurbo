# The Great Stink of 1858

In the summer of 1858, the most powerful city on Earth could not stand the smell of itself. London was the capital of an empire that covered a quarter of the globe. It had railways, telegraphs and the grandest new parliament building in the world. And running straight through the middle of it was a river of human waste.

That summer was unusually hot. The Thames dropped, the sun baked its muddy banks, and a stench rose off the water that no one in the city could escape. The newspapers called it the Great Stink. It would force Parliament to act faster than almost anyone thought possible, and it would give London a hidden piece of engineering that still works beneath its streets today.

To understand how London ended up here, you have to go back a few decades. For centuries, most Londoners relied on cesspits. Waste collected under houses and in back yards, and men called nightsoil collectors dug it out by hand and carted it away, often to be sold as fertiliser to farms outside the city.

It was a filthy system, but it had one accidental virtue. It kept most of the waste out of the river.

Then came a new invention that everyone agreed was a sign of progress: the flushing toilet. Water closets spread through wealthier homes in the early nineteenth century, and they became fashionable after the Great Exhibition of 1851. But a flushing toilet uses a lot of water, and all that water had to go somewhere. Cesspits overflowed. So the city began connecting houses directly to its sewers.

The problem was that London's sewers were never designed to carry sewage. They were built to carry rainwater. And they all led to the same place: the Thames.

At the same time, London was growing at a staggering rate. In 1800 it had around one million people. By the 1850s it had well over two million. Every one of them was now flushing into a river that was also, for many of them, a source of drinking water.

The Thames is a tidal river. Twice a day the tide pushed up from the sea, and twice a day it pulled back. That meant the waste dumped into the river did not simply flow away. It sloshed back and forth through the heart of the city, week after week.

The results were deadly. Cholera arrived in Britain in 1831 and returned in waves. The epidemic of 1848 and 1849 killed around fourteen thousand people in London alone. Another outbreak in 1853 and 1854 killed more than ten thousand.

At the time, most doctors and officials believed that diseases like cholera were spread by bad air, which they called miasma. Foul smells were not just unpleasant; they were thought to be the disease itself.

One doctor disagreed. In 1854, during an outbreak in Soho, a physician named John Snow mapped the cases and found them clustered around a single water pump on Broad Street. He persuaded the local authorities to remove the pump's handle. Snow argued that cholera was carried in contaminated water, not in the air.

He was right. But in the 1850s, very few people believed him.

The scientist Michael Faraday had also seen enough. In 1855 he took a boat out on the Thames, tore white cards into pieces and dropped them into the water to see how deep he could still see them. They disappeared almost as soon as they went under. He wrote to The Times that the river near the bridges was a fermenting sewer. The magazine Punch published a cartoon of Faraday handing his calling card to a filthy Father Thames.

Still, nothing much changed. Plans for new sewers had been drawn up, argued over and shelved again and again. The work would be enormous and expensive, and no one could agree on who should pay.

Then came the summer of 1858.

Parliament had only recently moved into its new home, the Palace of Westminster, built right on the riverbank. As the temperature rose in June, the smell from the Thames poured through its windows. Staff hung curtains soaked in chloride of lime, hoping to block the stench. It did not work.

Members of Parliament fled committee rooms with handkerchiefs pressed to their faces. There was serious talk of moving the courts and even Parliament itself out of the city. The Chancellor of the Exchequer, Benjamin Disraeli, described the river as a stygian pool, reeking with ineffable and unbearable horror.

For years, the problem had belonged to the poor neighbourhoods along the river. Now it was inside the building where the country's laws were made. And suddenly, the money appeared.

In the space of about eighteen days, Parliament passed a law giving the Metropolitan Board of Works the power to borrow three million pounds to clean up the Thames. It received royal assent at the start of August 1858. Years of delay were swept away in less than three weeks.

The job fell to the board's chief engineer, a man named Joseph Bazalgette.

Bazalgette was not a showman. He was a careful, methodical engineer who had already spent years studying London's drainage and drawing up plans that nobody had been willing to pay for. Now he had the authority and the money to build them.

His idea was simple to describe and enormously difficult to carry out. Instead of letting hundreds of sewers empty straight into the Thames in the middle of the city, he would intercept them.

He designed a set of huge tunnels, called intercepting sewers, that ran across London roughly parallel to the river. They would catch the flow from the old sewers before it reached the Thames and carry it east, away from the city, using gravity wherever possible. Where gravity was not enough, great steam pumping stations would lift the sewage to a higher level so it could keep flowing.

On the north side of the river, the sewage would travel to an outfall at Beckton. On the south side, it would go to Crossness. There it would be stored in reservoirs and released into the Thames on the outgoing tide, so the river would carry it out towards the sea instead of back into London.

The scale was enormous. The system included around eighty two miles of main intercepting sewers, fed by more than a thousand miles of smaller street sewers. It took hundreds of millions of bricks to build.

Bazalgette made two decisions that turned out to matter more than almost anything else.

The first was about size. He calculated how big the pipes needed to be for the population of London at the time. Then, the story goes, he doubled it, reasoning that they would only get one chance to do this properly. That margin let his sewers serve a city far larger than the one he was building for.

The second was about materials. Bazalgette used a relatively new kind of cement called Portland cement, which grows stronger when it is wet. He insisted it be tested rigorously, batch after batch. Many of the tunnels built with it are still in use today.

Some of the most visible parts of the project were not meant to be seen as sewers at all. Along the north bank of the Thames, Bazalgette built the Victoria Embankment, reclaiming land from the muddy foreshore. Inside it ran one of the main intercepting sewers. Above it he laid a new road and gardens, and alongside it ran the tunnel for an underground railway.

Even the pumping stations were designed to impress. Abbey Mills in the east of London, with its ornate ironwork and decorated interior, became known as the cathedral of sewage. Crossness, on the south bank, was opened in 1865 by the Prince of Wales.

In 1866, cholera returned to London one last time. This time the deaths were concentrated in the east of the city, in an area whose water supply had been contaminated, and which was not yet connected to Bazalgette's new system. For many observers, that pattern was hard to ignore. Water, not air, was spreading the disease, just as John Snow had argued twelve years earlier. Snow did not live to see it. He had died in 1858, the same summer as the Great Stink.

The system was not perfect. Moving the sewage downstream solved London's problem but created a new one near the outfalls. In 1878, a crowded pleasure steamer called the Princess Alice sank in the Thames close to where the sewage was released. More than six hundred people died, and many of the survivors had swallowed the polluted water. Over the following years the outfalls were upgraded, and London began treating its sewage rather than simply moving it.

Bazalgette was knighted in 1875, and he died in 1891. By then his tunnels were already part of the city's foundations, out of sight and mostly out of mind.

That is the strange legacy of the Great Stink. One of the most important public health projects in history happened in part because politicians could no longer bear the smell in their own building. The science that explained the problem was ignored until disaster forced a decision. And the solution worked so well that most people forgot it was there.

Bazalgette's margin lasted more than a century. But London kept growing, and during heavy rain the old combined sewers still overflowed into the river. So in the twenty first century, London built a new tunnel, the Thames Tideway Tunnel, running deep beneath the river to catch that overflow.

It was, in a way, the second chapter of the same story, and a reminder that the most important parts of a city are often the ones you never see.
