# Why is the night sky dark? Olbers' paradox explained

The night sky is dark because the universe is not infinitely old. If the cosmos were eternal, starlight from every line of sight would reach us, painting the sky white or bright. Yet, observations show a dark expanse punctuated by a few bright points, illustrating Olbers' paradox. 

The first resolution comes from the finite age of the universe. Light travels at a finite speed, so stars and galaxies beyond a certain distance have not yet had enough time for their photons to reach Earth. The visible universe is bounded by the cosmic light horizon, which limits the amount of starlight available to illuminate the sky. 

A second key factor is cosmic expansion. As the universe stretches, photons are redshifted— their wavelengths increase and energy decreases. Light from distant galaxies is shifted out of the visible band into radio or microwave frequencies, leaving the optical part of the spectrum largely unfilled. Additionally, intergalactic space is not perfectly transparent; scatter and absorption by interstellar dust further attenuate light, though this effect is minor compared to expansion. 

Together, the finite light‑travel time and the continual redshift of distant starlight explain why the night sky remains dark, resolving Olbers' paradox and confirming that our universe is both finite and dynamic.
